## Reading (opening only, OCR fair)

**Problem:** A hammer with its own spring cocked directly by the keys requires too much key force, and the paper-carriage escapement was impractical.

**Solution:** The same force that advances the paper carriage through an escapement wheel also cocks the impression hammer, which a key trips.

**How it works:** The hammer pivots on a standard, with its tail under the carrier bar and a pivoted dog that engages an escapement wheel. When the wheel turns in its arrow direction, the dog lifts the tail and presses the hammer back against its spring so it is set for a stroke. A second pivoted dog with a catch holds the wheel at the limit; a spring keeps its other arm depressed. A key lever lifts that arm, which disconnects the first dog and releases the hammer to strike. The carrier bar teeth gear into a pinion on the escapement wheel shaft, so the carriage drive both spaces and sets the hammer.

**Features:** [Spring-powered blow](../features/imp-spring-energy.md), [Escape-wheel escapement](../features/esc-escape-wheel.md), [Prints and spaces in one stroke](../features/esc-print-and-space.md), [Pawl-and-rack escapement](../features/esc-pawl-rack.md), [Line-end bell](../features/esc-bell.md), [Impression adjustment](../features/imp-impression-adjust.md)

**Parts:** hammer; spring; tail; dog; escapement wheel; catch; key lever; carrier bar; pinion; stop

**Improves on:**

- earlier hammers with a separate key-cocked spring: cocking is done by the carriage drive, not key force
