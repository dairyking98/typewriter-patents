## Summary

**Kind:** a part or mechanism (any kind of machine) · **Subject (patent classes):** Arrangements of keyboards, e.g. key button disposition

**In the English translation of the patent’s own words:** The object is a keyboard on which Japanese written in rōmaji can be typed easily, accurately and fast.

**How it works (reading):** Put all Japanese consonants on the left hand and all vowels and other letters on the right, arranged in home, upper and lower rows so voiced consonants sit under their unvoiced partners.

## Reading (full text, OCR good)

**Problem:** The standard and the Nippon-siki keyboards give rōmaji typists many awkward successive same-hand strokes, uneven finger loads, errors and fatigue.

**Solution:** Put all Japanese consonants on the left hand and all vowels and other letters on the right, arranged in home, upper and lower rows so voiced consonants sit under their unvoiced partners.

**How it works:** Left home row carries unvoiced consonants W S K T H, the row above N R M P L, the row below voiced Z G D B under S K T H. The right home row carries the vowels U O A I E, the upper row Y, long ô and û and common signs, and the lower row letters unused in Japanese (X C V F Q J). The layout is based on letter frequency and successive-stroke tables so most strokes alternate hands. It is produced on a standard machine just by changing the positions of types and keys.

**Features:**

- **Selection:** [Full keyboard, a key per character](../features/sel-keyboard-full.md)
- **Typebars:** [Typebar action](../features/bar-type-action.md)
- **Uses:** [Special scripts and characters](../features/use-special-script.md)

**Parts:** keyboard; home row; upper row; lower row; guide key

**Numbers:** alternate-hand strokes 91.1 % vs 45.3 % standard and 88.3 % Nippon-siki; awkward strokes 2.6 % vs 37.8 % and 9.5 %; home row load 71 %; right hand 55 %, left 45 %; 26 letters in three rows

**Improves on:**

- standard (QWERTY) keyboard: far fewer awkward successive strokes
- Nippon-siki keyboard: loads fingers more evenly and has fewer awkward strokes

## Description (English translation of the Japanese text, opening)

> Nature and object of the invention. In typing, so as to have as many as possible of the easiest strokes, those alternating between the hands, the letters are divided into two groups for the left and right hands: the left hand gets all the consonants used in the national language; the right hand gets all the vowels that combine with those consonants, and the other letters and signs. To make as rare as possible the awkward successive strokes by the same hand, and to make good use of the typist's awareness of letters and fingers, the keys of each hand are divided into three groups: home row, upper row and lower row. The left-hand consonants are divided into three groups: (1) the unvoiced letters that have a voiced counterpart, (2) the voiced letters, (3) the other consonants. Group 1, the most frequent, goes on the home row; group 3, next in frequency, on the row above; group 2, the voiced letters, on the row below, each under its unvoiced partner on the home row so that the same finger strikes both.
