## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** A low-cost typewriter needs a light, even key touch across the whole keyboard and an exact stop of the type segment so the impression is sharp, without a full set of typebars.

**Solution:** A single type segment with capitals on one side and small letters on the other is rotated and tilted by a pressing keyboard lever until a slanted-edge sliding plate meets the depressed key stem, which stops it at the right position; a hammer then prints through a ribbon.

**How it works:** Finger keys are set closely in rows on a keyboard that is itself a lever; pressing it down turns a pinion along a rack, which drives a quadrant lever, and via a toothed sleeve and bevel pinion turns the curved type segment. A sliding plate with three slanted edges moves under the keyboard with it and stops against the stem of whichever key was pressed, which disconnects a pawl and freezes the segment at the matching row position. A checking disk with holes and three rods splits keys into inner and two outer sections so the segment is also shifted in or out along its axis, and a separate side key reverses the toothed sleeve through toggles to swap between small and capital letters. Cams on the spring pinions keep an equal resisting tension through the whole keystroke. A hammer on a U-shaped slide that travels with the segment strikes the type against the paper through a ribbon held along the whole line. The segment and slide are pulled across the paper step by step by a cord and sheave with a rack-rim and double pawl, from a spring, and tripped back at the end of the line.
