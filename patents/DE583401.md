## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** In earlier electric cylinder typewriters the electromagnet that lifts the type cylinder to another row only moves for some keys, so the magnet cannot also be used to do other work such as turning the cylinder.

**Solution:** One electromagnet is energised on every keystroke and its armature, through a coupling rod set by key-driven rails, both lifts the six-row type cylinder by the needed number of rows and rotates it to the selected letter through a wedge between two opposed toothed racks.

**How it works:** The type cylinder has six rows of types. Two shift keys raise the cylinder shaft directly to the third or fifth row; letter keys in the second half of the keyboard also need a one-row lift. Each letter key lever moves a pawl and bracket that turn one of two rails, which closes the magnet circuit and positions a coupling rod so the armature stroke either lifts the cylinder one row or not. The armature stroke also drives a wedge down between two rack bars with graded teeth; a setting lever raised by the pressed key stops each rack at a different point, so the wedge is displaced left or right by a key-dependent amount and a gear sector turns the cylinder to the character. The cylinder is then struck against the paper roller, and a bracket releases the pawl so the rails return even though the key is still held.
