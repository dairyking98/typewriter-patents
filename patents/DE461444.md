## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** In earlier keyed striking-cylinder typewriters the angular setting comes from a common intermediate axis that springs back to zero after every stroke, so the cylinder cannot keep its position for successive letters, and the intermediate member limits how many keys fit.

**Solution:** Give each pair of keys a deformable four-bar linkage with two outer stops that rotate the cylinder and a central stop that fixes it, so the linkage returns on key release but the cylinder stays where it was set.

**How it works:** A key swings an intermediate axis, which shifts a rod forming one side of a vertical four-bar linkage whose horizontal bar carries two outer stops and one central stop. An outer stop pushes one side of a horizontal deformable quadrilateral, swinging a rocker arm that turns a toothed sector and gear, rotating the type cylinder until the rocker arm meets the central stop, which holds it. The same bar nose then pushes a cross bar that tilts the frame carrying the cylinder axis, which strikes the type against the paper. Three keys per set (small, capital, character) also lift the cylinder axially by 0, one, or two steps through a bar and angle lever, so the cylinder has three rows. A toothed wheel engaging a single fixed tooth locks the position at impact, and the 15th linkage with no rotation is the rest position.

**Numbers:** 84 keys; 15 four-bar linkages (14 with three stops, 1 central only); three type rows on the cylinder; capital shift raises the cylinder half the stroke of the character shift (1x versus 2x); cylinder rotates up to 180 degrees either way

**Improves on:**

- earlier keyed striking-cylinder typewriters with a common intermediate axis: cylinder keeps its set angle between strokes and many more keys fit, because each linkage works independently
