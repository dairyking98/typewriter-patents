## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** Existing type-cylinder typewriters need many levers, pins and (if electric) many magnets, are heavy and costly, and return the cylinder to a middle position after every print.

**Solution:** A single drive lever, pushed down by any key, sets the type cylinder angle through a comb rail, prints, and spaces the carriage, with an optional single electromagnet assisting.

**How it works:** Key levers rest on rods or wires in a punched base plate. Pressing a key pushes its lever end down through linkage onto the drive lever, and also pushes up a sprung pin beside the key. As the drive lever descends, a toggle rod swings a comb rail (drop bar) into position so the raised pin enters a toothed gap of different depth; this shifts the rail sideways by an amount specific to the key. The rail moves a toothed segment that turns a small gear on the cylinder axle, so the type cylinder rotates directly from its previous angle without returning to centre. At the bottom of the stroke an angled arm swings the cylinder bearing against the platen to print, and a pawl and lever advance the carriage one tooth on its rack. The cylinder has six rows of types in two-row groups; keys of one group lift or lower the cylinder axis by a wedge pawl to change row without a shift key, and two shift keys change group. In the electric version one magnet, closed by a contact when any key is depressed, supplies the drive-lever force.
