## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** Hammond wanted a simpler, more responsive, lower-effort and lower-friction machine in which the key levers, escapement, hammer and carriage work together harmoniously.

**Solution:** A complete Hammond machine in which a rubber type segment is oscillated either way by two driving levers acting through a combined stop-arm and locking member, while one key stroke also releases a spring-powered escapement that spaces the carriage and cocks and fires the impression hammer.

**How it works:** Uniform key levers on a knife-edged rib raise stop pins and push two horizontal driving arms; the farther a key is from the median line, the more the driving lever moves and the farther the hard-rubber, three-row type segment turns left or right on its shaft, until a stop pin halts the stop-arm. The stop-arm drives, arrests, returns, arrests at rest and locks the driving arms (so keys on opposite sides cannot act together). Two shift keys raise the segment shaft, with adjustable stops, to bring the capital or figure rows under the hammer, and a cam lever can lock a shift key down. A rocking feed frame under the key levers pivots an escapement pawl so the carriage mainspring, working through a rack, pinion and escape wheel, advances one tooth while a weaker hammer spring is released to strike the paper against the type through a ribbon; hammer re-cocking happens on key release. A space key only operates the escapement while a hooked lever checks the hammer. Two ribbon spools are fed by a worm shaft and ratchet pawl from the feed frame, a flexible apertured shield keeps the ribbon off the paper, and the carriage has a line-space thumb lever, a release rod that disengages the pawl from a ratchet wheel, and a bell on the hammer arm struck by a carriage-mounted bell hammer.
