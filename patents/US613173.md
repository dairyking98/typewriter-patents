## Reading (full text, OCR good)

**Problem:** Hammond-type machine with a single type carrier needed easier touch, speed, accuracy and simpler construction while keeping the printing visible.

**Solution:** A slit, molded celluloid type plate with six rows is moved sideways by two alternating driver/stop arms, raised by a key-lever bank for row selection, and hit by a spring-retarded hammer against a fixed flat platen that the paper carriage shifts up and down.

**How it works:** Four banks of keys; each key lever has two adjusting screws that set the exact lateral stop position of the type plate through two transverse levers acting on two opposed arms of one rock-shaft, one arm driving while the other acts as stop (no return spring; the plate stays where the last stroke left it). Left-hand keys additionally raise the type plate one row via a flexible spring arm and rocking frame, so the six rows are reached by two vertical positions of the plate combined with the shift of the paper carriage up or down. The shift key raises or lowers the carriage rail so the fixed longitudinal flat platen faces a different row pair (caps, lower case, figures). A hammer on a rock-shaft is driven through a spring-loaded arm that retards the blow to damp stop vibration; the same shaft swings the type frame and ribbon forward toward the platen just before the hammer strikes, and advances the ribbon spool ratchet each stroke. Letter spacing is by a ratchet bar with a jointed pawl worked by the hammer shaft, with a separate space key and a lift-off for large carriage travel; line feed uses a friction-held pawl lever.

**Numbers:** six types per finger; six rows on the plate; four banks of keys; hammer face spans five rows

**Improves on:**

- rigid-hammer typewriters with lost-motion: a spring-retarded hammer shaft damps stop vibration without losing force
- roller-platen machines: a fixed flat longitudinal platen with a carriage that shifts vertically
