## Summary

**Kind:** type-element machine · **Subject (patent classes):** Typewriters or selective printing mechanisms characterised by the mounting, arrangement or disposition of the types or dies with types or dies mounted on carriers rotatable for selection the plane of the type or die face being parallel to the axis of rotation, e.g. with type on the periphery of cylindrical carriers

**In the patent’s own words:** My aim is to provide a machine which will turn out work exhibiting that clearness and uniformi ty of impression and accuracy and regularity of spacing found at present only in printers press-work.

**How it works (reading):** A spring-driven pair of type wheels designates the next character on each key stroke and prints the previously designated one, with letter spacing proportional to each character's width.

## Reading (full text, OCR fair)

**Problem:** In ordinary typewriters the key that designates a character also prints it, so a wrong key is recorded before it can be corrected; also fixed-width characters do not give the clearness and regular spacing of printers' press-work.

**Solution:** A spring-driven pair of type wheels designates the next character on each key stroke and prints the previously designated one, with letter spacing proportional to each character's width.

**How it works:** Two type wheels (lower case on the upper, upper case on the lower) sit on a common keyed hub on a shaft driven by a spring fusee through a cord, rewound by a hand lever. Pressing a key throws up one stop pin in a dial; at the same time a locking yoke engages a toothed wheel on the shaft and an arm depresses the previously raised pin, so the shaft is held while an impression plunger, driven through a toggle by a rotating disk, prints the earlier character. The yoke then releases and the shaft turns until a spring-cushioned arm hits the newly raised pin, bringing the new character to the print point. A second pin dial in the carriage spacing train is set by a friction-clutch arm whose stopping point is fixed by the length of a projection on each key (wider characters have shorter projections), so the carriage advances by the width of the character printed. The upper-case key raises the wheels with a latch that drops them after one stroke, and moves a cross-bar so uppercase characters get more space. A push-bar rod stops the plunger striking when correcting an error, an inking roller circulates round an ink cylinder when the hand lever is pulled, and the same lever returns the carriage and feeds the paper line.
