## Reading (full text, OCR good)

**Problem:** Threaded stem-key shanks work loose and turn in their sockets so the key legends go askew; earlier split-shank or tenon-and-mortise fixings were complex and costly.

**Solution:** Give the stem-key a tapering shank that is wedged into a matching tapering seat in the socket pivoted on the key lever.

**How it works:** A socket is pivoted on the front of each key lever by a pin through its forked lower end. The socket has a tapering vertical seat and the key shank ends in a matching taper. The shank is slipped in, turned and pushed down with a screwing motion until it wedges, after which it is hard to pull out and cannot turn, so the legends stay square to the front edge of the machine. The shown machine is the Caligraph; the taper may alternatively be on the socket side.

**Features:**

- **Selection:** [Full keyboard, a key per character](../features/sel-keyboard-full.md)
- **Type element:** [Typebars](../features/elem-typebar.md)
- **Frame and body:** [Few parts, low cost](../features/frm-few-parts.md), [Adjustment and wear take-up](../features/frm-adjustment-wear.md)

**Parts:** stem-key; key head; tapering shank; socket; tapering seat; key lever; pivot pin

**Improves on:**

- US Yost threaded-socket stem-keys: taper wedge replaces a screw thread that loosens
- US Yost patents of 1883-09-18 and 1886-02-23 (split-shank spring jaw links): simpler and cheaper
