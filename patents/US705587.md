## Reading (full text, OCR good)

**Problem:** Key-wheel typewriters needed elaborate connections between the keys and the type wheel, and could not use a standard keyboard layout; the wheel also had to return positively and stay aligned when printing.

**Solution:** Each key lever carries a cam-slotted selecting head that, through a vibrating rod, turns one common selecting lever, which rotates the type wheel by rack and pinion while the wheel rocks to the platen.

**How it works:** Each key lever ends in a head with a uniquely shaped cam slot. Pressing a key raises its head and the slot walls push a vibrating rod (one for each half of the keyboard), which turns the selecting lever through connecting rods and a crank lever. The lever's ball end sits between guides on a sliding rack bar; the rack turns a pinion on the type-wheel spindle, so the wheel turns to the character (never more than 180 degrees, since left and right keys turn it opposite ways). The wheel and spindle sit in a nutating rocker, so the ball joint allows free rocking. The same key motion lifts a cross-head that pulls pitmen down, rocking the wheel about 60 degrees to the platen; slotted pitmen delay the rocking until selection is done, and the cam slot shape then locks the position. A star wheel and knife-edge wedge hold the alignment, springs and the cam slot walls return the wheel, and shift keys slide the spindle axially to the capital and figure bands.
