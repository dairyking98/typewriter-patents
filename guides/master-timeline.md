# The master timeline

Index, type-element and typebar machines grew up side by side, and this page puts all three into one chronology from 1829 to the 1950s, era by era. Each era has a short account of what was happening, a table of landmark machines in the three lines, the features that appeared or became standard, and the counts from this library.

## How to read this page

The typewriter took three main forms. The library sorts every dated machine into one of them, or into "unknown" when the Lexikon entry does not say enough.

**Index machines.** You choose a character by moving a pointer, dial or lever over a chart (the "index"), then make one print stroke. One type carrier and one print action make them cheap, small and simple, but slow. See [What is an index typewriter?](index-typewriters.md), [How index machines work](how-index-machines-work.md) and [German index typewriters](german-index-typewriters.md).

**Type-element machines.** You use a keyboard, but all the characters sit on one moving carrier: a wheel, cylinder, plate or curved shuttle that is turned or slid to the right character before a hammer or a swing of the element prints it. The Hammond and the Blickensderfer are the classic examples. See [Type-element typewriters](type-element-typewriters.md).

**Typebar machines.** Every character (usually two, upper and lower case) sits on the end of its own lever, the typebar, and each key swings its own bar to a common printing point. This is the familiar office typewriter, from the Sholes & Glidden to the Olympias and Underwoods. See [Typebar typewriters](typebar-typewriters.md).

The [timeline browser](../explore/timeline.md) shows the same material interactively. You can draw lanes by country or by kind of machine (or put everything in one lane), filter by kind, and click any year to see the machines made and the patents granted that year. The [features view](../views/typewriter-features.md) and the decade-by-decade [timeline view](../views/timeline.md) hold the underlying lists.

**About the counts.** All counts here come from this library, not from a census of everything ever made. The 1,089 dated machines are taken from Dingwerth's *Lexikon*, which leans towards German machines (503 of them, against 407 American). The patents are US patents to 1951 and German patents to 1950, but the US set is dense only up to 1915: after that it holds just a handful a year, while the German set is thin before 1920 and dense after it. So a jump from American to German numbers around 1920 is partly a change of source, not only a change in the world. Patent summaries were made by machine from OCR text and can be wrong. A "first patent" for a feature is the earliest one in the library whose text deals with it, which is not always the true invention. Check the patent page and drawings before relying on a detail.

## Before 1870: inventors, one machine at a time

Before 1870 there was no typewriter industry, only inventors. Almost every machine of this era is a single model or a handful, built by its designer and never sold. All three lines already appear, often in odd mixtures. [William Burt's typographer](../machines/burt.md) of 1829 ([USX5581](../patents/USX5581.md)) is an index machine: a type segment on a swinging lever is brought to the printing point by hand, and one impression lever prints and moves the paper on. [Charles Thurber](../machines/thurber.md) in 1843 ([US3228](../patents/US3228.md)) mounted one type rod per character around a horizontal wheel that was whirled round to the printing point. The library files it with the typebar machines, since each character has its own rod. [Oliver Eddy's machine](../machines/eddy-s-typewriter.md) of 1850 ([US7771](../patents/US7771.md)) moved a grid of type bars in two directions over an inking hole.

One type per lever or rod was already the most common idea. [Xavier Progin's machine](../machines/nicht-fabrikma-ig-hergestellt-1833.md) of 1833 in France is the earliest the library files as a typebar machine, followed by [Pierre Foucauld](../machines/nicht-fabrikma-ig-hergestellt-1851.md) (1851), [Alfred Ely Beach](../machines/nicht-fabrikma-ig-hergestellt-1856.md) (1856), [Odkolek](../machines/odkolek.md) in Prague (1860), [Peter Mitterhofer](../machines/nicht-fabrikma-ig-hergestellt-1864.md) in the Tyrol (1864) and [Giuseppe Ravizza](../machines/nicht-fabrikma-ig-hergestellt-1867.md) in Italy (1867). The Danish [Malling Hansen](../machines/malling-hansen.md) writing ball (1867) also counts as a typebar machine here: each character has its own type rod, set radially in a half-sphere so that all of them point at one printing spot. It is one of the few machines of the era that was actually sold, in very small numbers. The true type elements were fewer: [Charles Wheatstone's](../machines/wheatstone.md) segment machine in England (1856), [Guillemot's](../machines/guillemot.md) three type wheels in France (1859) and [John Pratt's](../machines/pratt.md) machine (1863, [US81000](../patents/US81000.md), a flat type plate moved in two directions under a hammer).

The United States leads the dated machines (11), but many early entries have no clear country. Patenting is sparse: 26 US patents in the library for the whole period, never more than four in a year.

## 1870–1885: the first commercial typewriters

The modern typewriter begins with the typebar machine that [Christopher Latham Sholes](../machines/nicht-produziert-1868.md) and his partners worked on from the late 1860s ([US79265](../patents/US79265.md), 1868), and that E. Remington & Sons put into production as the [Sholes & Glidden](../machines/e-remington-sons-1874.md) in 1874. It was an upstrike machine: the typebars hit the underside of the platen, so the typist could not see the line being written. It printed capitals only and cost $125. The Lexikon records only modest sales. Remington's [1879 model](../machines/e-remington-sons-1879.md) by Yost and Brooks had 76 characters on a QWERTY-type keyboard and sold well. Its rival, the [Caligraph](../machines/american-writing-machine-co-1880.md) of the American Writing Machine Co (1880, designer Yost), avoided a shift by giving every character its own key.

The typebar was not yet the obvious winner. James Hammond patented his type-element machine in 1880 ([US224183](../patents/US224183.md): notched key levers turn a type segment and pins hold it exactly in place for the hammer). The [Hammond 1](../machines/hammond-1.md) of 1884 used a curved type shuttle, could change typefaces and sold well. Lucien Crandall's [Crandall No. 4](../machines/crandall-no-4.md) (1881, [US251338](../patents/US251338.md)) used an interchangeable type sleeve. In the cheap market, index machines appeared in numbers for the first time: the [Hall 1](../machines/hall-1.md) (1880, $40), the [Columbia Index 1](../machines/columbia-index-1.md) (1884, $25, Charles Spiro) and the tiny [Sun Index 1](../machines/sun-index-1.md) ($12). Germany's first factory-made machines were index machines too: the [Guhl & Harbeck machine](../machines/guhl-harbeck-1882.md) of 1882, the [Hammonia](../machines/hammonia-hansen-patent.md) and the rare [Westphalia](../machines/westphalia.md) (both 1884).

## 1885–1900: the American boom

These fifteen years were the most inventive in the typewriter's history. US patents in the library climb from 39 in 1885 to a peak of 204 in 1890, and dozens of American firms launched machines. All three lines boomed at once.

The typebar line split into rival layouts. Understrike machines in the Remington tradition stayed dominant in offices: the [Smith Premier](../machines/smith-premier-typewriter-co-1888.md) (1888, 76 keys, one per character), the [Yost](../machines/yost-writing-machine-co-1889.md) (1889) and the Densmore. The [Oliver 1](../machines/oliver-1.md) (1893) found another route to visible writing, with U-shaped type bows that swung down onto the top of the platen from towers either side. The decisive change came with front-strike machines, which put the line in view as it was typed: the [Daugherty](../machines/daugherty-typewriter-co-1892.md) (1892) and above all [Franz Wagner's machine](../machines/wagner-typewriter-co-1896.md) of 1896, a front-strike QWERTY design that sold well (the design that, as is generally told, became the Underwood). German typebar making began with copies: the [Frister & Rossmann](../machines/frister-rossmann-1-4.md) of 1892 was a Caligraph copy, and the [Empire / Adler 7](../machines/empire-adler-7.md) (1898, W. P. Kidder's push-rod typebars) sold in large numbers.

Type elements had their best years. The Hammond grew into the [1B Ideal](../machines/hammond-1b-ideal.md) and [Hammond 2](../machines/hammond-2-universal.md). George Blickensderfer's small type-wheel machines, the [Blickensderfer 5](../machines/blickensderfer-5-b.md) above all, sold in very large numbers at a fraction of a typebar machine's price ([US621742](../patents/US621742.md) describes the wheel lock). The [Munson](../machines/munson-1.md) (1890) used a type cylinder, the [Commercial Visible](../machines/commercial-visible-1.md) (1898) a type wheel and hammer.

Index machines peaked in variety. Some were simple office machines, like the [World 1](../machines/world-1.md) (1886, $8) and the [Odell 1](../machines/odell-1-gerade.md) (1887, $15; [US399205](../patents/US399205.md)). Others were near-toys, like the [Simplex](../machines/simplex-type-writer.md) ($2.50) and the [Dollar Typewriter](../machines/dollar-typewriter.md) of 1890, which really did sell for a dollar. The German [Kosmopolit](../machines/kosmopolit.md) (1888, Guhl & Harbeck) bridged the lines with a keyed type plate. At the far end, the [Chinese typewriter (A)](../machines/chinese-a.md) of 1899 was an index machine with a wheel of about 4,000 characters, probably never produced.

The type element was still the inventors' favourite on paper, even as the typebar took the market.

## 1900–1920: the visible typebar takes over, and Germany arrives

Between 1900 and 1920 the front-strike, visible-writing typebar machine with a four-row QWERTY keyboard and a single shift became the standard office typewriter. The features data shows it: [visible writing](../views/typewriter-features.md#visible-writing), the [shift key](../views/typewriter-features.md#shift-key-case-shift) and [QWERTY](../views/typewriter-features.md#qwerty-keyboard) all became standard on new machines in the 1900s. In America the [L. C. Smith & Bros](../machines/l-c-smith-bros-typewriter-1904.md) (1904, designed by Carl Gabrielson) sold in large numbers, [Royal](../machines/royal-typewriter-co-1905.md) began in 1905, and the [Corona](../machines/corona-typewriter-co-inc-1912.md) of 1912 made a light folding portable typebar machine a mass product. The [Noiseless](../machines/noiseless-typewriter-co-1915.md) (1915, Kidder again) chased quiet. US patenting peaked at 407 in 1910.

The bigger change in the library is Germany. A wave of German firms began making typebar machines, many of them with designs bought or copied from America: [Ideal](../machines/ideal-a1-1.md) (1900, Seidel & Naumann), [Adler 8](../machines/adler-8.md) (1902), [Stoewer](../machines/model-1-original.md) (1903), [Continental](../machines/continental-standard.md) (1904, Wanderer, "very large quantity"), [Torpedo](../machines/torpedo-2.md), [Mercedes](../machines/mercedes-1.md) (1907), [Erika](../machines/erika-1-2.md) (1910, a portable) and [Triumph](../machines/triumph-1.md) (1910). In this era Germany passes the USA in the Lexikon's count, 155 machines to 147, and 127 typebar machines to 107. Part of that lead reflects the Lexikon's German focus.

Type elements still held real niches. [Blickensderfer](../machines/blickensderfer-8.md) kept selling small type-wheel machines and built an [electric](../machines/blickensd-electric-1.md) one in 1901. The [Postal](../machines/postal-1.md) (1902) was a cheap three-row portable, and the [Hammond Multiplex](../machines/hammond-multiplex.md) (1913) let the typist switch between two type shuttles with a turn of a knob. Most index machines were now small and cheap. The great exception was AEG's [Mignon](../machines/mignon-2.md) (1904 onwards), Friedrich von Hefner-Alteneck's pointer-and-cylinder machine ([US914272](../patents/US914272.md), and probably [DE149308](../patents/DE149308.md), whose inventor is not named in the library's copy). It sold very well at 100 marks. Toy and pocket index machines spread too: the [Liliput](../machines/liliput-a.md) (1907), the [Famos](../machines/famos.md) children's machine (1910, 3.50 marks) and the [Virotyp](../machines/virotyp-b.md) (1914, France). Far from Europe, the [Nippon](../machines/nippon.md) of 1917 was a Japanese index machine with about 2,380 separate types.

## 1920–1950s: the typebar standard, the portable, and the toy

After the First World War the typewriter was a mature product. In the library this era is told mostly through German patents and machines, and the US patents nearly vanish. German patenting jumps to 114 in 1920 and peaks at 236 in 1923. Most of those patents refine the typebar machine: line-spacing clutches, ribbon reversing, carriage switching, two rows of typebars on one guide ([DE518524](../patents/DE518524.md), Winterling, 1931) and power-driven typebars ([DE678362](../patents/DE678362.md), 1939). Machine output stays high through the 1920s and 1930s, collapses during the Second World War (13 German patents in 1945) and recovers after 1948.

Typebar machines made up almost all of the new models: office machines from [Rheinmetall](../machines/rheinmetall-modell-8.md), [Orga](../machines/orga-privat-1-5.md), [Olympia](../machines/olympia-7.md) and Adler in Germany, and from Remington, Royal and Underwood in the USA. Portables were the growth market: [Erika 5](../machines/erika-5.md) (1927, "very large quantity"), the [Klein-Triumph](../machines/modell-1-klein-triumph.md) (1928) and the Klein-Adler, Klein-Continental and Klein-Kappel. The features data shows patents on [portable machines](../views/typewriter-features.md#portable-machines) at their highest in the 1920s (46) and 1930s (41). Electric typebar machines arrived in earnest with the [Electromatic](../machines/electromatic-typewriters-inc-1930.md) of 1930, later IBM's, and the [IBM Executive](../machines/ibm-executive.md) of 1952 added proportional spacing.

The type element shrank to specialist work. The [Hammond Varityper](../machines/hammond-varityper.md) (1927) sold well as a composing machine with changeable fonts, and the [Sampson Permagraph](../machines/sampson-permagraph.md) (1929) was an electric type-wheel machine, very few made. The index machine became the cheap home and toy machine. In Germany: the later [Mignon 4](../machines/mignon-4.md) (1924, "large numbers"), the [Gundka](../machines/gundka-3.md) (1924, 30 marks, [DE417060](../patents/DE417060.md)), [Gerda 2](../machines/gerda-2.md) ([DE393929](../patents/DE393929.md)), the slide-rule-sized [Karli](../machines/karli-1-einreihig.md) (1927, [DE440774](../patents/DE440774.md)) and Olympia's Mignon-derived [Plurotyp](../machines/olympia-plurotyp.md) (1933). In America: Louis Marx's tin [Dial De Luxe](../machines/dial-de-luxe.md) (1934) and [Junior Typewriter](../machines/junior-typewriter.md) (1935). For the very large character sets of Chinese and Japanese, the index principle remained the practical answer, as in the [Chinese (B)](../machines/chinese-b.md) (1920, about 2,500 types) and the [Shimada](../machines/shimada.md) (1938, over 3,000).

**Why typebars won.** A typebar machine gives every character its own key, lever and type. That makes it complicated and costly to build, but fast to use: the typist never waits for a wheel or shuttle to turn and settle, and with ten fingers the keys can overlap in time. Once front-strike designs like the Wagner put the writing in view, and the four-row QWERTY keyboard with one shift became the norm in the 1900s, the typebar machine did everything an office needed. It also became the machine that typists were trained on and that firms bought by the thousand, which made it cheaper still. Type-element machines were inventive and often better at one thing, such as changing typefaces on the Hammond or light weight on the Blickensderfer, but they lost the office market. In this library type elements fall from 31 new machines in the 1900s to 2 in the 1930s.

**Why index machines persisted.** The index machine never competed on speed. It competed on price, size and simplicity, and those advantages never went away. From the Dollar Typewriter of 1890 to Marx's tin toys of the 1930s, and from the Mignon to the Gundka and Karli, it filled the markets a typebar machine was too expensive or too big for: the home, the traveller, the child, and the languages with thousands of characters. That is why its count stays at a steady ten to thirty machines a decade from the 1880s to the 1930s, long after it had lost the office.

**What the type element led to.** The idea of one interchangeable carrier for every character, explored in Crandall's sleeve, Hammond's shuttle and Blickensderfer's wheel, came back after this library's period in IBM's Selectric "golf ball" of 1961 (listed here as the [IBM 72](../machines/ibm-72-selectric.md)) and then in daisy-wheel printers and typewriters.
