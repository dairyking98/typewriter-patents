# German index typewriters, 1880–1950

German inventors patented index typewriters from the early 1880s onward: machines in which the writer picks a character with a pointer, dial, stylus or lever over a chart and then makes a single print stroke. This guide follows that line from the hand-built pointer and type-wheel machines of the 1880s and 1890s, through AEG's Mignon and its two-axis pointer field, to the cheap, toy and teaching machines of the 1920s and 1930s.

## About the sources

About 400 of the library's roughly 3,900 German patents have a "Reading" (problem, solution, how it works). These were written by machine from Google's translation of German OCR and can be wrong. Dates of the oldest patents are often estimated from the patent number, many early records name no inventor, and titles starting with "~" come from the text. Each patent page links the original scan at DEPATISnet.

Machine facts come from the machine pages, drawn from Dingwerth's *Kleines Lexikon historischer Schreibmaschinen* and *Geschichte* volumes. A patent linked to a machine with only "medium" or "low" confidence is a guess from names and dates, and is flagged as such here.

## The 1880s–1890s: pointers, dials and wheels

### The first machines

The earliest German index machines in the Lexikon date from 1884. The [Westphalia](../machines/westphalia.md) of E. W. Brackelsberg carried its 86 or 87 characters on a straight type rule, printed upward, and took its ink from carbon paper. It cost 300 marks and sold in very small numbers. The firm [Guhl & Harbeck](../companies/guhl.md) made the [Hammonia](../machines/hammonia.md) of the same year, classed as a pointer-bar machine.

The patent record starts slightly earlier. [DE21564](../patents/DE21564.md) (1883, date estimated) sets out the basic idea: one lever on a sliding carriage holds a type rod, a pointer key drops into a rack to set the character, and pressing the lever prints and steps the carriage. Its addition, [DE23653](../patents/DE23653.md), keeps the index plate still (before, it went down with the type bar on every letter, so the eye lost it) and prints only if the pointer really sits between two teeth of the chart.

### Rubber type, metal type, cheap machines

Many 1880s patents aim openly at a simple, cheap machine, and many use rubber type to get a clean print from a light stroke:

- [DE33343](../patents/DE33343.md) (1885): a sheet-metal disc cut into radial tongues, each with a vulcanized rubber type, set by a lever pushed into a notched two-row letter table.
- [DE35428](../patents/DE35428.md) (1886): a wheel of springy arms, each ending in a key with metal or rubber type underneath and its letter on top.
- [DE41127](../patents/DE41127.md) and [DE42124](../patents/DE42124.md) (1887): a rubber type segment swung by a pointer and pressed through a window, the whole print head stepping along the line.
- [DE78210](../patents/DE78210.md) (1895): soft rubber type on a metal segment, with a pin driven into a hole under the chosen type during the stroke to hold it in place.

### The print stroke sets the register

A recurring idea is that the writer only has to point near the right character: the print stroke itself pulls the element into exact position, as a tooth, knife or cone enters a notch on the element before the type reaches the paper. Examples:

- [DE32539](../patents/DE32539.md) (1885): pressing the type-wheel handle drives a fixed tooth into a ratchet wheel on the type wheel. Notches of different depth also give each letter its own carriage advance.
- [DE52906](../patents/DE52906.md) (1890): a rubber type ring under a bell; on the downstroke a pin on the bell enters a hole that widens upward, centring the type.
- [DE54936](../patents/DE54936.md) (1890) states the idea most clearly. A V-shaped tooth on the print-lever axis drops into a V-toothed wheel coupled to the type wheel and snaps the nearest character into place, "so the pointer only has to be near the letter".
- [DE72355](../patents/DE72355.md) (1894): a hand-turned wheel with a letter dial on its face; pressing the button first engages a toothed ring under the wheel, then inks and prints.

The idea runs on through the Mignon and the 1920s–30s patents below (feature tag: [selector locking](../features/sel-selector-locking.md)).

### Finger rings and one-handed writing

[DE54315](../patents/DE54315.md) (1890) begins from a complaint: on pointer machines one hand sets the pointer and the other works a separate print lever, which is slow and shuts out anyone with one arm. Its answer is a spring-mounted finger ring on the pointer handle. One finger sets the letter, presses a long fingerboard to print (swinging the carriage against the type wheel) and presses a rail to space, while a nose locks the wheel. Its addition [DE57454](../patents/DE57454.md) (1891) adds capitals: pulling out an extension of the finger ring lifts the wheel along its axis to a second ring of capitals.

### Elements that carry their own index

Several designs print the index on the element itself rather than on a separate chart: the type-and-letter keys of [DE35428](../patents/DE35428.md), the dial on the face of the wheel in [DE72355](../patents/DE72355.md), and the ring of [DE63641](../patents/DE63641.md) (1892), with letters on its outer edge and matching labels on its inside.

Selection by a pin in a hole is another recurring form ([pointer in a hole](../features/sel-pointer-hole.md)). [DE47755](../patents/DE47755.md) (1889) has a stylus pressed into one of 72 holes over a type disc, with wider holes for wider letters. The depth of the stroke sets the carriage advance. [DE78296](../patents/DE78296.md) (1895) uses an adjusting pin in a perforated index plate over an elastic type plate.

### Firms and the 1890s production machines

The name Soennecken appears on both sides of the record. The Lexikon lists a [Soennecken](../machines/soennecken.md) index machine of 1890 with a linear type rule and 88 characters that was never sold. [DE48923](../patents/DE48923.md) (1889), whose summary names Soennecken, describes a sledge with two rows of exchangeable type, set by a handle pressed into hollows in an alphabet board. The match is plausible but unconfirmed.

Other 1890s machines were real products. The [Edelmann](../machines/edelmann.md) (1897), a type-wheel machine with 84 characters and double shift, is recorded at about 20,000 copies, at 85 or 125 marks. The [Volksschreibmaschine](../machines/volksschreibmaschine.md) ("people's typewriter", 1898, Friedr. Rehmann) had 76 characters on a type wheel and sold for 75 marks. Its designers are given as Beyer and Markworth, probably the same pair as the unsold [Beger & Markworth](../machines/beger-markworth.md) prototype of 1897 under a spelling variant, though that is a guess. The [Büttner](../machines/buttner.md) and [Kochendörfer Index](../machines/kochendorfer-index.md) of 1896 were prototypes only.

[DE57812](../patents/DE57812.md) (1891) shows a different use: a pocket cipher machine 8 cm across, meant for officers, with a type disc turned by a removable spindle.

## The Mignon and the pointer-field cylinder

### Sell's patent

[DE149308](../patents/DE149308.md), which the Lexikon credits to Louis Sell (granted 22 December 1901; the library's record date is 1903), is the confirmed basis of AEG's [Mignon](../machines/mignon-1.md). It starts from a problem with keyless type-cylinder machines: setting and printing take a lot of hand movement, yet if the movement is made small, the index chart becomes too small to read.

The solution has three parts:

- **One handle for everything.** The cylinder overhangs the end of the housing on an axis held in a frame that swings about a horizontal pin. A rack carrying the handle and pointer meshes with a gear on the cylinder axis. With the handle raised, the writer sets the pointer over a checkerboard index plate; pressing down prints.
- **Two-axis selection.** The cylinder has several rows of type. Moving the pointer one way turns the cylinder and moving it the other way slides it along its axis, so every character has its own field on the chart. There is no shift: capitals, small letters and figures are all simply fields on the board (84 characters on Mignon 1).
- **Magnified pointer travel and positive register.** A pantograph-like linkage makes the pointer travel much further than the cylinder moves, so the chart can be large while the hand motion stays small. As the frame descends, one knife enters a comb and another enters a star wheel to fix the exact position, which is the 1890s register idea applied to two axes.

A felt roller inks the type, and a button on the left advances the paper.

### AEG and Hefner-Alteneck

The machine pages name Friedrich von Hefner-Alteneck as designer of the Mignon. The [Hefner-Alteneck Tasterapparat](../machines/hefner-alteneck-tasterapparat.md) of 1902 is recorded as a prototype built on Sell's patent. A US patent in his name, [US914272](../patents/US914272.md) (1909), covers the same idea: a knob on a pantograph-magnified linkage moves the type roller a short distance while the pointer travels widely over a large index plate.

The Mignon sold steadily for over two decades. Mignon 1 (1902) is recorded at only 50 to 200 copies at 80 marks, with an ink roller. [Mignon 2](../machines/mignon-2.md) (1904) moved to a ribbon and sold very well at 100 marks. [Mignon 3](../machines/mignon-3.md) (1913) and [Mignon 4](../machines/mignon-4.md) (1924) are both recorded in large numbers. In 1933 [Olympia](../companies/olympia.md), the successor to AEG's typewriter business, made the [Plurotyp](../machines/olympia-plurotyp.md), which the Lexikon calls a copy of the Mignon, with 76 characters (its price is given in Swiss francs).

### Variations on the pointer field

Once the Mignon was established, other German patents worked on the same layout:

- [DE332718](../patents/DE332718.md) (1921, Heinrich Vester) carries several fonts on a pointer-set cylinder as spring-loaded type pistons. Struck from above by a hammer, it could mark thick objects such as bound books, boxes or wood.
- [DE377030](../patents/DE377030.md) (1923) notes that one field per character makes a large board (84 fields). It puts two keys on each field, the second of which also turns or slides the cylinder, so which key is struck decides small or capital.
- [DE393930](../patents/DE393930.md) (1924, Robert Langguth) refers directly to "Mignon-type" machines and shrinks the chart to 24 fields for a 96-type cylinder. Buttons on the pointer add a fixed extra rotation, a fixed axial step, or both.
- [DE450547](../patents/DE450547.md) (1927, Karl Gommel) mounts the chart and the cylinder on one swinging frame for a narrow machine.
- [DE651112](../patents/DE651112.md) (1937): the key lever first pulls the alignment knife fully into its comb, and only then releases a pre-tensioned impression spring.

## The 1920s–1930s: small, cheap, toy and teaching machines

After the First World War the German index machine moved mostly into toys, children's machines and cheap pocket devices. Earlier precedents exist: the [Famos](../machines/famos.md) of Gustav Tietze (1910) is described as a children's type-wheel machine at 3.50 marks, and the [Trebla](../machines/trebla.md) (1910) as a miniature machine.

### Gundka and Paul Muchajer

The [Gundka-Werk](../companies/gundka.md) appears in its name variants as a group of "united tin-toy factories" (*Vereinigte Blech-Spielwaren-Fabriken*), with Paul Muchajer as inventor. The Lexikon lists [Die kleine Gundka](../machines/die-kleine-gundka.md) (1924) as a pointer-and-type-wheel index machine, alongside the [Gundka 3](../machines/gundka-3.md) and [Gundka 5](../machines/gundka-5.md), which it classes as single-key type-wheel machines. The [Muchajer patents](../people/muchajer-p.md) are linked on the Gundka 3 and 5 pages, not on the kleine Gundka page. Several are mainly about how the element is mounted and located:

- [DE329783](../patents/DE329783.md) (1920, medium link): a quick-change type-wheel mount. A nut-and-washer fixing was slow to change and, if loose, let the wheel turn and print wrong letters. Instead a spring holds the wheel up against a cross wedge pin in a shallow groove; push down, turn and lift to remove it.
- [DE386555](../patents/DE386555.md) (1923, medium): a stylus over a three-ring character plate. Moving it sideways turns the wheel, and moving it inward drives a wedge that lifts the wheel to the second or third row.
- [DE417060](../patents/DE417060.md) (1925, high): a pointer-set type cylinder on a swivel plate. One key moves it axially to one of three rows.
- [DE446207](../patents/DE446207.md) (1927, high): fine adjustment at the moment of impression. As the cylinder swings toward the platen, a guide disc enters one of three row slots in a fixed guide piece and a toothed disc meshes with its toothed edge, locking rotation and row together.
- [DE446282](../patents/DE446282.md) (1927, high): cylinders built from flat type strips with notches cut between the characters, so that rolled on a mandrel they fold into a polygon with flat type faces instead of a blurred curved face.
- [DE413386](../patents/DE413386.md) (1925, high): a typewriter frame made of sheet metal, as one would expect from a tin-toy maker.

### Gerda and Georg Emig

The [Gerda 1](../machines/gerda-1.md) (1919) and [Gerda 2](../machines/gerda-2.md) (1921) of Georg Emig and Walter Oehmke were type-wheel index machines with 81 characters and double shift, rated very rare and recorded as selling very few. Gerda 2 is linked with high confidence to Emig's [DE393929](../patents/DE393929.md) (1924). It objects that a pointer and chart far from the wheel, joined by linkage, set it imprecisely and bend on impact, so it puts the pointer and three-row chart directly above the wheel axis. Sideways pointer motion turns the wheel through a toothed segment, a move to another row swings the wheel frame, and a key swings the frame down to print.

### Other toy and teaching machines

- The [Gescha](../machines/gescha.md) (1920) of Max Schmid and the [Junior GSN](../machines/junior-gsn.md) (1920) of Otto Schmidt are toy type-wheel machines with 65 characters and single shift; the Junior is recorded as selling well. [DE544900](../patents/DE544900.md) (1932) by a Max Schmid concerns locking the type wheel on children's typewriters with two spring-loaded rollers that drop between the types. The machine pages do not link it to the Gescha, so the connection is only a guess from the name.
- [Karli 1](../machines/karli-1-einreihig.md) and [Karli 2](../machines/karli-2-zweireihig.md) (1927–28) by Karl Dietrich used individual types; Karli 1 cost 42 marks. Their pages link [DE440774](../patents/DE440774.md) only with low confidence. That patent describes a slide-rule-sized machine with spring-loaded metal types in a perforated double rail. A pin on the print button drops into a slot to centre each type.
- The [Carissima](../machines/carissima.md) (1934) was a type-wheel index machine in a Bakelite case. Its designer field mentions an Edgar Hoffmann; [DE609811](../patents/DE609811.md) (1935) by Edgar Hoffmann mounts the type wheel on a slide plate that moves straight to the platen, with a pin entering a fixed comb so the character cannot shift. The link rests only on the name.
- [DE570377](../patents/DE570377.md) (1933) is a teaching typewriter with a key-driven type wheel in which a nose on each key lever stops the switching bridge from overshooting.
- [DE576185](../patents/DE576185.md) (1933, Alfred Knorr) is a children's machine whose button swings a type sector carrying a tape of type plates, then presses it to the platen, where a pawl locks the letter.
- Stylus machines: [DE469128](../patents/DE469128.md) (1929) and [DE625422](../patents/DE625422.md) (1936), both by Sebastian Loewenbuck, stop a type wheel against a pen placed in a letter board or under a struck key. [DE577948](../patents/DE577948.md) (1933, Stylotype) uses a ball-tipped stylus over a notched board that is itself pressed down to print.
- Pocket machines: [DE434835](../patents/DE434835.md) (1926) is a label writer set by a pin placed in a hole in the type wheel, and [DE694372](../patents/DE694372.md) (1940, Paul Karstedt) a spring-steel type tape struck with a thimble hammer worn on a finger.

## Notable inventors and firms

- **[AEG](../companies/aeg.md)**, Louis Sell and [Friedrich von Hefner-Alteneck](../people/von-hefner-alteneck-f.md): the Mignon, the most successful German index machine, continued as the Olympia Plurotyp.
- **[Paul Muchajer](../people/muchajer-p.md)** of the Gundka-Werk: the largest group of 1920s patents on mounting and locating type wheels and cylinders.
- **[Georg Emig](../people/emig-g.md)**: the Gerda machines.
- **[Oskar Picht](../people/picht-o.md)**: the [Picht Index](../machines/picht-index.md) (1907, Herde & Wendt), a type-wheel machine with 84 characters and double shift at 48 or 65 marks.
- **[Sebastian Loewenbuck](../people/loewenbuck-s.md)**, **[Edgar Hoffmann](../people/hoffmann-e.md)**, **[Robert Langguth](../people/langguth-r.md)**, **[Karl Gommel](../people/gommel-k.md)** and **[Heinrich Vester](../people/vester-h.md)**: individual inventors of 1920s–30s pointer and stylus patents.

## Most interesting German patents

- [DE23653](../patents/DE23653.md) (1883): keeps the index plate still, and prints only when the pointer sits between two chart teeth.
- [DE32539](../patents/DE32539.md) (1885): type wheel locked by the print stroke, with notches of different depth giving letter-width spacing.
- [DE33343](../patents/DE33343.md) (1885): slotted sheet-metal disc with rubber type tongues, set over a two-row notched letter table.
- [DE47755](../patents/DE47755.md) (1889): stylus in one of 72 holes, the stroke depth setting the letter's spacing.
- [DE54315](../patents/DE54315.md) (1890): finger-ring pointer for one-handed setting, printing and spacing.
- [DE54936](../patents/DE54936.md) (1890): V-tooth snap that pulls the nearest character into register.
- [DE57812](../patents/DE57812.md) (1891): 8 cm pocket cipher machine for officers.
- [DE149308](../patents/DE149308.md) (1903, Sell): the Mignon, a two-axis pointer field with magnified pointer travel and knife-and-comb register.
- [DE332718](../patents/DE332718.md) (1921, Vester): pointer cylinder with spring type pistons, for writing on thick objects.
- [DE393929](../patents/DE393929.md) (1924, Emig): pointer and chart placed directly over the type wheel (Gerda 2).
- [DE393930](../patents/DE393930.md) (1924, Langguth): a 24-field chart for a 96-type Mignon-style cylinder.
- [DE329783](../patents/DE329783.md) (1920, Muchajer): push-and-turn quick-change type-wheel mount.
- [DE446207](../patents/DE446207.md) (1927, Muchajer): guide piece that locks cylinder rotation and row at impression.
- [DE609811](../patents/DE609811.md) (1935, Hoffmann): type wheel on a straight slide with a comb lock, so the character does not shift.
- [DE694372](../patents/DE694372.md) (1940, Karstedt): spring-steel type tape and finger-thimble hammer.
