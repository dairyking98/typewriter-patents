# Type-element typewriters

A type-element typewriter has a keyboard like an ordinary typewriter, but instead of a type bar for each character it carries every character on one part (a wheel, shuttle, cylinder, sleeve, segment or sphere) that moves to bring the chosen character to the printing point. This guide follows the main lines from Crandall, Hammond and Blickensderfer to the German machines of the 1900s and the spheres of the patent record, and explains how the machines worked and what makers gained and lost by putting all the type on one piece.

Machine facts come from the library's machine pages, drawn from Dingwerth's *Lexikon*, which class 84 machines as type-element machines. Patent facts come from the library's patent summaries; 534 patents are classed under type wheels, cylinders, discs, plates, spheres and similar carriers, a count that also includes index machines using the same carriers. The summaries were made by machine from OCR text and can be wrong, so check a patent's page and drawings before relying on a detail. Most patent-to-machine links in the library are guesses from the inventor's name; their confidence (low, medium or high) is given below.

## Keyboard, one element

The library sorts every machine into one of three kinds:

- **Typebar machines** give each character (or pair of characters) its own pivoted bar, swung by its own key to a common printing point. See [Typebar typewriters](typebar-typewriters.md).
- **Index machines** use one type carrier but no full keyboard: you aim a pointer, dial or stylus at a chart, then make a separate print stroke. See [What is an index typewriter?](index-typewriters.md) and [How index machines work](how-index-machines-work.md).
- **Type-element machines** combine the two. One key press both chooses and prints a character, as on a typebar machine, but the key does not throw type at the paper. It turns, slides or tilts the single element until the right character faces the paper, and then the element or a hammer makes the impression.

The hard part is in the middle. A typebar key only swings its own bar. A type-element key must turn a shared element by an amount that differs for every key, stop it exactly, hold it still for the strike and let it go in time for the next key. Most of the patents below are about doing that quickly and accurately.

Some unusual machines look as if they belong here but do not. The Danish [Malling-Hansen](../machines/malling-hansen.md) writing ball (1867), with its type rods, and the [Oliver](../machines/oliver-1.md) machines (from 1893), with their U-shaped "type bows", give each character its own carrier, so they count as typebar-family machines here (see [Typebar typewriters](typebar-typewriters.md)).

## Before 1880

The single moving carrier is older than the commercial typewriter. William Burt's 1829 patent [USX5581](../patents/USX5581.md) brings a type segment on a swinging lever to one printing point by hand. Charles Thurber's 1843 machine ([Thurber](../machines/thurber.md), [US3228](../patents/US3228.md)) has a horizontal wheel carrying a key for each character, whirled round until the wanted key can be pressed down; each character has its own type rod, so it too counts as typebar-family here, but its turning wheel anticipates the single element. The Lexikon also lists a type-segment machine by [Charles Wheatstone](../machines/wheatstone.md) (1856) and a French machine with piano keys and three type wheels by [Guillemot](../machines/guillemot.md) (1859). None was sold. Eugene Cadmus's [US127739](../patents/US127739.md) (1872) points far ahead: a type wheel spins under power and the paper is lifted against it at the instant the chosen type comes round.

## Crandall's type sleeve

Standard histories (outside the library) count Lucien Crandall's machine as the first single-element keyboard typewriter to be sold. His 1881 patent [US251338](../patents/US251338.md) puts one interchangeable **type sleeve**, a short cylinder with nine rows of nine characters, on an oscillating printing lever. Each key rocks a shaft through its own differently shaped cam, sliding the sleeve lengthwise by the right amount; keys for the side rows also twist it by a set angle. A toothed segment locks the sleeve, and the printing lever swings it against the platen. Lower case, capitals and figures are separate fields, reached by turning the sleeve with a hand slide.

The Lexikon dates the [Crandall No. 4](../machines/crandall-no-4.md) to 1881 (84 characters, ribbon, double shift, 75 dollars, "not very many" sold) and the three-row [Crandall No. 3](../machines/crandall-no-3.md) to 1893; the library links US251338 to them with low and medium confidence respectively. Crandall's [US342244](../patents/US342244.md) (1886) replaces the ribbon with an inking roller. Others copied the layout: [US447720](../patents/US447720.md) (Wilder, 1891) and the German [DE60102](../patents/DE60102.md) (1891) swing "Crandall-style" cylinders down onto paper that lies flat in view.

Charles Spiro's [US322495](../patents/US322495.md) (1885) slides a square type sleeve until a stop meets the pressed key, and turns it by hand to change type style. Spiro founded the Columbia Typewriter company (outside the library), but in this library the Columbia and Victor names belong to index type-wheel machines, such as the [Columbia Index 1](../machines/columbia-index-1.md) (1884) and [Victor Index](../machines/victor-index.md) (1894), and to later typebar machines, not to keyboard element machines.

## Hammond's type shuttle

James B. Hammond's patents of 1880–1883 show him trying several elements before settling on the curved **type shuttle**, a segment of a ring with the characters on its outer face. In [US224183](../patents/US224183.md) and [US232402](../patents/US232402.md) (1880) notched key levers act as cams that turn a type segment, and a pin raised by each key catches a stop arm. [US249930](../patents/US249930.md) (1881) and [US253475](../patents/US253475.md) (1882) use type wheels instead. [US290419](../patents/US290419.md) (1883) is a complete machine with a rubber segment swung either way, the same stroke spacing the carriage and firing the hammer, and [US290420](../patents/US290420.md) (1883) splits the characters over two segments that each swing one way only. All are linked to the [Hammond 1](../machines/hammond-1.md) with low confidence.

The Hammond 1 of 1884 had wooden keys, 90 characters, ribbon and double shift, cost about 100 dollars and sold well. Its key feature is the hammer: the shuttle stands behind the ribbon and paper, and a spring-driven hammer strikes the paper forward against the type, so the shuttle itself can be light. Later patents refine this. [US501504](../patents/US501504.md) (1893) mounts a thin hard-rubber type strip on a rigid steel anvil, because hollowed shuttles were weak and could rebound and split letters. [US780102](../patents/US780102.md) (1905) adds a reversible carriage for right-to-left scripts and a turret hammer with a face for Braille, and [US825967](../patents/US825967.md) (1906) makes the shuttle a thin, easily changed ring; these are linked to the Hammond 12 with low confidence.

The [Hammond](../companies/hammond.md) line runs through the [1B Ideal](../machines/hammond-1b-ideal.md) (1891), [2 Universal](../machines/hammond-2-universal.md) (1895), [12 Universal](../machines/hammond-12-universal.md) (1905), [Multiplex](../machines/hammond-multiplex.md) (1913) and [Varityper](../machines/hammond-varityper.md) (1927). "Ideal" and "Universal" name the keyboard: Hammond's curved DHIATENSOR layout or a QWERTY-type one. The [Keystone](../machines/keystone.md) (1899, [Keystone Typewriter Comp](../companies/keystone.md)) and [Sterling](../machines/sterling-1.md) (1905, [Sterling Typewriter Comp](../companies/sterling.md)) were shuttle-and-hammer machines in the same pattern; both sold few.

## Blickensderfer's type wheel

George C. Blickensderfer took the opposite approach: his light **type wheel** is itself thrown at the paper. His patents are linked to the machines with high confidence. In [US410662](../patents/US410662.md) (1889) the three-row wheel has a geared hub between two racks; each key has its shoulder at a different height, so it pushes the first rack down by a different distance and turns the wheel to its character, the second rack stops the turn, and further pressure carries the wheel to the platen. [US432296](../patents/US432296.md) (1890) moulds the wheel as one hollow, braced hard-rubber piece. [US459093](../patents/US459093.md) (1891) turns the wheel and moves it toward the platen in one motion, with an eccentric ratchet whose graded teeth stop it and a second pawl that keeps it from bouncing onto the wrong character.

The machines from [Blickensderfer Manufacturing Comp](../companies/blickensderfer.md) began with the front-strike [Blickensderfer 2](../machines/blickensderfer-2.md) (1891, not sold). The [Blickensderfer 5](../machines/blickensderfer-5-a.md) (1893, top strike, ink roller, 84 characters, 160 marks) and its successor [5 (B)](../machines/blickensderfer-5-b.md) (1895) sold in "very large" numbers, followed by the [7](../machines/blickensderfer-7.md) (1897), the French [Dactyle](../machines/dactyle.md) (1900), the [8](../machines/blickensderfer-8.md) (1907), the aluminium [6](../machines/blickensderfer-6.md) (1910), the [Nocoblick](../machines/nocoblick.md) for music notation (1912) and the QWERTY-type [9](../machines/blickensderfer-9.md) (1914). The [Blickensd. Electric 1](../machines/blickensd-electric-1.md) (1901, 125 dollars, few sold) was among the first electric typewriters; [US656085](../patents/US656085.md) (1900, medium) has each key clutch a motor shaft in for exactly one turn. A light wheel suited this, since the motor only had to move a few grams of rubber.

## Other American and British machines

From about 1887 to 1910 many smaller makers built a cheaper keyboard machine around one element:

- **Mercury.** The English miniature [Mercury 3](../machines/mercury-3.md) (1887, [Mercury](../companies/mercury.md)) is tied with high confidence to Frederick Myers's [US419209](../patents/US419209.md): a clock spring spins the wheel when a key releases a brake, and pins on per-row discs stop it.
- **Munson and Chicago.** The [Munson 1](../machines/munson-1.md) (1890) and [Chicago 2](../machines/chicago-2.md) (1897, 35 dollars, [Munson](../companies/munson.md)) used a type cylinder and print hammer. Samuel Seifried's [US446394](../patents/US446394.md) (high) has thirty keys that slide and turn a splined sleeve; [US548726](../patents/US548726.md) (high) splits the characters over two cylinders to strike hard enough for carbon copies.
- **Gardner.** The [Gardner](../machines/gardner.md) (1890, [Karl Lipp](../companies/lipp.md)) had 14 keys with six characters each. In [US463859](../patents/US463859.md) (medium) each key presses a stepped lever to a different depth, and the cylinder stops against the pressed key stem itself.
- **Travis.** The [Travis](../machines/travis.md) (1905, [Philadelphia Typewriter Comp](../companies/philadelphia.md)) rests on Byron Brooks's [US461865](../patents/US461865.md) (1891, high): a gutta-percha wheel spun either way by gear bars, stopped by the key, lifted to its other rows by two shift keys.
- **Commercial Visible.** The [Commercial Visible 1](../machines/commercial-visible-1.md) (1898, [Commercial Visible Typewriter Co](../companies/commercial.md)) is linked (medium) to Richard Uhlig's [US593789](../patents/US593789.md), which wedges the gearing because separate wheel locks wore and let the wheel shift.
- **Bennett.** The tiny [Bennett](../machines/bennett.md) (1910, 18 dollars, [Elliott-Fisher](../companies/elliott.md)) matches Charles Bennett's [US888457](../patents/US888457.md) (medium): wheel, pinion and stop rack sit on a slide that the key first turns and then drives forward to print.
- **Cheap portables.** The [Postal 1](../machines/postal-1.md) (1902, [Postal](../companies/postal.md)), [Eagle](../machines/eagle.md) (1902), [Official](../machines/official.md) (1901) and [McCool](../machines/mc-cool.md) (1903) were type-wheel machines at 20 to 25 dollars, all sold in small numbers; the British [Moya Visible 1](../machines/moya-visible-1.md) (1903, [Moya](../companies/moya.md)) used a vertical cylinder.

Electric versions appeared too: the [Kent](../machines/kent.md) (1892, not sold), the [Ennis](../machines/ennis.md) (1901) and the [Sampson Permagraph](../machines/sampson-permagraph.md) (1929).

## German machines of the Mignon era

In Germany the best-known single-element machine of the 1900s, AEG's [Mignon 1](../machines/mignon-1.md) (1902), was an index machine with a type cylinder; see [German index typewriters](german-index-typewriters.md). Keyboard element machines were made in the same years, mostly in small numbers:

- The [Imperial](../machines/imperial-dt.md) (1900, 250 marks, [Kochendörfer](../companies/nh.md)), a top-strike type-wheel machine with ink roller. Heinrich Kochendörfer's [US711727](../patents/US711727.md) (medium) says hard-striking wheels wore their type, and tilts the wheel against the paper through a spring for a softer blow.
- The [electric type-cylinder machine](../machines/hefner-alteneck-electric-type-cylinder-machine.md) of about 1902–03 by Friedrich von Hefner-Alteneck for [AEG](../companies/aeg.md), its cylinder struck by an electromagnet; Dingwerth's factory history says it was rejected.
- The lightweight [Phönix 1](../machines/phonix-1.md) (1906, 90 marks, [Grosser](../companies/maschinenfabrik.md)) and the [Thürey 1, 2](../machines/thurey-1-2.md) (1907, [Thürey](../companies/thurey.md)) with print hammer.
- The two-row [Helios 1, 2](../machines/helios-1-2.md) (1909, 98 marks, triple shift, [Krebs](../companies/dt.md)), continued as the [Helios 3](../machines/helios-3.md) ([Kanzler](../companies/kanzler.md)) and the [Helios-Klimax](../machines/helios-klimax.md) (1914, [Ney](../companies/ney.md)).
- The [Soblick](../machines/soblick.md) (1912, [Soblick](../companies/soblick.md)), worked by compressed air. Maximilian Soblik's pneumatic patent [US818200](../patents/US818200.md) (1906) is not linked to it in the library; the name suggests the same inventor, but that is a guess.
- The [Nüse](../machines/nuese.md) (1904, not sold) with type segments and hammer; [US1030005](../patents/US1030005.md) (1912, Meyer and Nuese) may belong to it, though the library makes no link.

German patents kept the idea going after the market turned to typebars. Hermann Teichmann's [DE327616](../patents/DE327616.md) (1920) gives all 44 keys the same short stroke and turns and lifts an eight-field cylinder through slotted cam levers; Hermann Duchrow's [DE728836](../patents/DE728836.md) (1942) sets a small type head by a wedge on each key and throws it at the platen with a spring, so the blow no longer depends on the finger.

## Spheres and later elements

A type sphere, where one tilt and one turn reach any character, appears in only four of the library's read patents ([ELEM:sphere](../features/elem-sphere.md)). Daniel Stover's [US409073](../patents/US409073.md) (1889) tilts a spherical type block on a universal joint at its centre and then pushes it straight down; Francis Richards's [US892891](../patents/US892891.md) (1908) uses a three-tier spherical turret, turned by a friction-driven shaft and tilted by a rock frame. Eduard Thürey's [US741739](../patents/US741739.md) (1903) is also classed under spherical carriers (linked to the Thürey machines with low confidence). Other inventors tried a semi-cylinder on a twisted axle ([US1098605](../patents/US1098605.md), 1914), flexible type bands ([ELEM:band](../features/elem-band.md)), and keys weighted 1, 2 and 4 that add to select any of 126 types ([DE683955](../patents/DE683955.md), 1939).

By the late 1940s the element patents are about printing units for business and punch-card machines (IBM's [DE839130](../patents/DE839130.md), 1948) and cheaper wheels moulded around a toothed metal disc ([DE850530](../patents/DE850530.md), 1949). The library's patents end around 1951; IBM's golf-ball [Selectric](../machines/ibm-72-selectric.md) of 1961, which made the single element standard in offices, came after (well-known history).

## Why one element: gains and costs

**Gains.** *Changeable type*: lifting off a wheel or shuttle changes typeface, language or script, which nobody could do with 80 typebars; [ELEM:interchangeable](../features/elem-interchangeable.md) is one of the commonest element features (137 patents). *Fewer parts and a lower price*: Crandall, Gardner and Myers all name a simpler, cheaper machine as their aim ([FRM:few-parts](../features/frm-few-parts.md)), and the portables of 1900–1910 sold for about a quarter of the Hammond 1's 100 dollars. *Light weight*: a rubber wheel made the Blickensderfer 6 and the Bennett truly portable ([FRM:compact-portable](../features/frm-compact-portable.md)). *Alignment*: every character prints from the same place, so letters cannot drift out of line as worn typebars do; Stover's patent stresses that every type arrives with the same key travel. *Visible writing*: with one small element the line can stay in view, the stated aim of [US447720](../patents/US447720.md) and [DE60102](../patents/DE60102.md) ([FRM:writing-visible](../features/frm-writing-visible.md)).

**Costs.** *Speed*: the element must turn, stop, strike and return for every letter, and two keys cannot overlap as two typebars can; Otto Pfeiffer's [US1024745](../patents/US1024745.md) (1912) calls type-cylinder machines slow and wasteful of power. *Wear and play*: the stop and lock take a blow on every stroke, which Uhlig's [US593789](../patents/US593789.md) and Hammond's [US501504](../patents/US501504.md) both try to cure; [ELEM:alignment-lock](../features/elem-alignment-lock.md) gathers 182 patents on holding the type still. *Weak impression*: a light rubber element made poor carbon copies, the problem behind Seifried's [US548726](../patents/US548726.md). *Complex motion*: turning and lifting one element by a different amount for each key takes more ingenuity than a typebar linkage.

## How the keys position the element

Every keyboard element machine has to turn a key press into a rotation, and often a lift or slide, of a key-specific size ([SEL:differential-drive](../features/sel-differential-drive.md)). The usual methods:

- **Graduated keys**: shoulders or lugs at different heights move a shared rack or plate by different amounts ([US410662](../patents/US410662.md)), or press a stepped lever to different depths ([US463859](../patents/US463859.md)).
- **Lever ratios**: keys meet a common lever at different distances from its pivot ([US290420](../patents/US290420.md)).
- **Cams and slopes**: each key has its own cam or slot slope, rocking a shaft by its own angle ([US251338](../patents/US251338.md), [US557728](../patents/US557728.md), [DE327616](../patents/DE327616.md)).
- **Stops**: the element is driven by the key, a spring ([US419209](../patents/US419209.md)) or a motor ([US656085](../patents/US656085.md), [US892891](../patents/US892891.md)) and halted by a pin the key raises ([US224183](../patents/US224183.md)) or by the key stem itself.
- **Locks**: a tooth, pin or ratchet then holds the element for the strike ([SEL:selector-locking](../features/sel-selector-locking.md), [ELEM:alignment-lock](../features/elem-alignment-lock.md)), as with Blickensderfer's paired pawls.

Many designs turn the element either way from a central rest, so no character is more than half a turn away; in Brooks's [US461865](../patents/US461865.md) the keys nearest the middle of the keyboard travel least and serve the types nearest the home point.

## How it prints, inks and shifts

**Printing.** In the Hammond pattern the element stays put and a **hammer behind the paper** drives paper and ribbon against the type ([IMP:hammer-behind](../features/imp-hammer-behind.md)), as also in the Munson, Commercial Visible, Keystone and Sterling. In the Crandall and Blickensderfer pattern the **element moves to the platen**, swung on a lever, pushed on a slide or tilted on a pivot ([IMP:element-to-platen](../features/imp-element-to-platen.md)). Later patents such as [US1150382](../patents/US1150382.md) (1915) cock a spring with the key and release it, so every letter is struck equally hard ([IMP:spring-energy](../features/imp-spring-energy.md)).

**Inking.** A single small element can be inked directly: Blickensderfer and Mercury machines use an **ink roller** that touches the type on its way ([INK:roller](../features/ink-roller.md)). Hammond and most hammer machines use a **ribbon** between type and paper ([INK:ribbon](../features/ink-ribbon.md)).

**Shift.** Rows on the element replace the typebar machine's shift. A three-row wheel or shuttle holds lower case, capitals and figures, and the shift key slides or lifts it along its axis to bring another row to the print point ([SHIFT:axial](../features/shift-axial.md), [SHIFT:triple](../features/shift-triple.md)). Crandall's sleeve was turned to a new field instead ([SHIFT:rotary](../features/shift-rotary.md)).
