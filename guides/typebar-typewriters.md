# Typebar typewriters

A typebar typewriter gives every character (usually a pair, small and capital) its own type bar, swung by its own key to one common printing point. This guide follows how that design solved the great problem of each era, from blind understrike writing to visible front strike, shift and keyboard, and how it became the standard office and portable typewriter, made in America and above all in Germany.

Machine facts come from the library's machine pages, drawn from Dingwerth's *Lexikon*. The library classes 806 of its 1,110 machines as typebar machines. All counts here are counts in this library, not a census, and the Lexikon leans towards German machines. Patent facts come from the machine-made summaries (US 1829–1951, German 1877–1950). Of these, 1,120 are marked as typebar patents and 190 of those have a full reading. Summaries made from OCR can be wrong, so check a patent's drawings before relying on a detail. A patent-to-machine link of low or medium confidence is a guess from names, and this guide says so. Many machine pages give only a maker and a year. Familiar model names added here, such as "Caligraph" or "Underwood", come from general typewriter history, not from the page.

## What makes a typebar machine

Pressing a key pulls, often through an intermediate lever, on a slender pivoted bar with the type at its tip. The bar swings and the type strikes the paper on the platen through an inked ribbon, and then the carriage moves on one space. Forty-odd bars are hung in an arc, the *type basket* or *segment*, so that every one lands on the same spot. Compare the other two families:

- An **index machine** has one type carrier and no full keyboard: you aim a pointer at a chart, then make a separate print stroke. See [What is an index typewriter?](index-typewriters.md).
- A **type-element machine** has a full keyboard, but all the type sits on one wheel, shuttle, cylinder or sleeve that turns to the right character. See [Type-element typewriters](type-element-typewriters.md).

A typebar machine has many more parts, each bar must be adjusted to land in line, and so it cost more to build. In return it is fast: nothing has to turn and settle before printing, and a trained typist's strokes can overlap. ## Where the type hits the paper

The library's strike field has three values. *Understrike (blind)* means the bars swing up and hit the underside of the platen (English-speaking collectors also call this "upstrike"). *Top strike* is the Lexikon's *Oberaufschlag*, in which the type comes down onto the top of the platen (collectors' "down-strike"). *Front strike* means the bars swing up from a segment in front of the platen and hit its front face.

### Understrike: fast, but blind

Christopher Latham Sholes and his partners patented their machine in 1868 ([US79265](../patents/US79265.md), [US79868](../patents/US79868.md)). E. Remington & Sons put it into production in 1874 as the [Sholes & Glidden](../machines/sholes-glidden-3.md), with 43 characters, capitals only, at $125. Its bars hung in a circle under the platen. The line being written was out of sight, and the typist lifted the carriage to read it. Remington's [1879 model](../machines/remington-2.md) by Yost and Brooks added a shift for 76 characters and sold well. James Whitcomb's ribbon-reverse patent [US309272](../patents/US309272.md) (1884) describes this class of machine exactly: keys throw the type "upward and against" the platen.

Understrike was still the commonest layout of the 1890s (41 of 87 typebar machines). Examples are the [Smith Premier](../machines/smith-premier-1.md) (1888), the [Yost](../machines/yost-1.md) (1889), the [Densmore](../machines/densmore-1.md) (1891) and the [Fay-Sholes](../machines/rem-sho-7.md) (1901). The Lexikon's note on the [Jewett (Germania)](../machines/jewett-3.md) of 1898 shows what blind writing meant in practice: the carriage was hinged so it could be tipped up to read the line. After 1905 understrike nearly disappears.

### Top strike: writing in view, the awkward way

Many early attempts to show the writing brought the type down onto the top of the platen. The library has 92 top-strike typebar machines. The earliest is [Xavier Progin's](../machines/progin.md) of 1833, followed by [Charles Thurber's](../machines/thurber.md) machine of 1843 with its type rods and the Danish [Malling-Hansen writing ball](../machines/malling-hansen.md) of 1867, whose rods point inwards like the spines of a ball. Most date from 1885 to 1910. Edward Horton's [US285265](../patents/US285265.md) (1883) angles the bars on concentric arcs so that the print stays visible. It is linked to the [Horton](../machines/horton.md) of 1885 only with low confidence. Other top-strike machines include Charles Spiro's [Columbia](../machines/bar-lock-1.md) (1887, sold in Britain as the [Bar-Lock](../machines/bar-lock-15.md)), the [Crown](../machines/crown-rund.md) (1887), Kidder's [Franklin](../machines/franklin-1-2.md) (1891) and the [Williams](../machines/williams-1-c.md) (1891). On the Williams, as general history describes it, the bars lay in front of and behind the platen and rose to strike its top. British examples are the [Fitch](../machines/fitch-2-b.md), [Salter](../machines/salter-5.md) and [Imperial](../machines/imperial-a.md) (1908). Flat-bed book typewriters such as the [Elliott-Hatch](../machines/elliott-hatch-1.md) (1897) printed downwards onto bound books. The most successful down-strike typebar machine was the Oliver. Its U-shaped type bows stood in two banks on either side of the platen and came down onto its top. Thomas Oliver's [US528484](../patents/US528484.md) (1894) states the aim of printing on the upper side of the platen so the work is "constantly exposed" to the operator (the link to the machines is low confidence). The library has ten top-strike Olivers, from the [Oliver 1](../machines/oliver-1.md) (1893, few sold) through the [Oliver 5](../machines/oliver-5.md) (1907, "large numbers") to the [Oliver 15/16](../machines/oliver-15-16.md) (1928), nearly all with double shift. Only the [1930 Oliver](../machines/oliver-portable-4.md) changed to front strike. The description of the bows is general history; the pages give only "type bow" and the strike. Top strike needed tall or crowded mechanisms, and after 1910 it gave way to front strike.

### Thrust action: type pushed, not swung

Wellington Parker Kidder slid the type straight at the platen. His [US471795](../patents/US471795.md) (1892) describes panel-shaped bars sliding endwise, with bevelled guides steering the type to the printing point. [US567241](../patents/US567241.md) and [US567242](../patents/US567242.md) (1896) drive the bar with a toggle that straightens at impression, aiming at a nearly noiseless machine that prints by "dwelling pressure" rather than a blow. This is the action of the Canadian [Williams Manufacturing](../machines/empire-1-a-canada.md) machines (1892, in general history the Empire) and of the [Empire / Adler 7](../machines/adlerwerke-vorm-heinr-kleyer-ag-1898.md), built by Adler from 1898. The library links Kidder's patents to both with medium confidence. The Adler 7 cost 300 gold marks and was advertised for its visible writing. About 3,000 were sold by 1901 and over 250,000 by about 1930. The library has 41 push-rod typebar machines, mostly Adlers, with copies such as the [DWF](../machines/dwf.md) (1922). Kidder kept working on quiet writing. His [US1122764](../patents/US1122764.md) (1914), linked with high confidence to the [Noiseless](../machines/noiseless-1.md) of 1915, accelerates and then slows the bar for a firm impression from a light touch. The [Torpedo Lautlos](../machines/torpedo-lautlos.md) (1933) used the Remington Noiseless 8 mechanism.

### Front strike: the answer that stuck

In a front-strike machine the bars lie in a flat semicircle in front of and below the platen. Each bar swings up and back through a slot in the segment and strikes the front of the platen, where the typist can see it. The earliest examples in the library are the [Prouty](../machines/prouty-hynes.md) and [Condé](../machines/conde.md) of 1885, both made in very small numbers. The decisive machines came in the 1890s. The [Daugherty](../machines/daugherty.md) (1892, $75) has its type-action patents [US553153](../patents/US553153.md) and [US556460](../patents/US556460.md) linked with high confidence. Franz Wagner's [front-strike machine of 1896](../machines/underwood-2.md) and its [1900 successor](../machines/underwood-4.md) sold "very many", and his type-bar mounting patent [US704152](../patents/US704152.md) is linked with high confidence. In general history this is the Underwood, built for John T. Underwood from 1897.

Wagner's layout was widely followed. The Lexikon calls the [Germania Visible](../machines/germania-visible.md) (1900) "after Underwood" and the [Regina 1](../machines/regina-2.md) (1904) Underwood-style. It says the [Torpedo 2](../machines/torpedo-2.md) (1907), [Urania 1](../machines/urania-1.md) (1909) and [AEG model 1](../machines/model-1-2-bronze-aeg-3.md) (1912) used the Underwood-type segment. Seidel & Naumann's [Ideal A1](../machines/ideal-a1-1.md) (1900) struck from the front with its own linkage, and the firm took up Wagner's linkage in the [Ideal B](../machines/ideal-b.md) (1912). In America the [L. C. Smith](../machines/l-c-smith-2.md) (1904) and [Royal](../machines/royal-grand.md) (1905) followed. Remington switched with its [1908 model](../machines/remington-10.md), usually identified as the Remington 10. The features view dates [visible writing](../views/typewriter-features.md#visible-writing) as standard on new machines from the 1900s.

## Shift and keyboard

A typebar carries one, two or three types, so a full set of capitals, small letters and figures needs either many keys or a shift. The Lexikon's shift field shows three answers:

- **Full keyboard, no shift.** Small and capital letters have separate keys. There are 51 such typebar machines, all from 1880 to 1914: the [Caligraph](../machines/caligraph-1.md) (1880), the Smith Premier, the Yost, the Jewett and the Columbia. The [Smith Premier of 1908](../machines/smith-premier-10.md) still sold in large numbers with one key per character.
- **Single shift.** Four rows of keys, two types per bar, and one key that moves the platen. Byron Brooks's [US202923](../patents/US202923.md) (1878) aims to print capitals and small letters "without having duplicate keys and type-bars". The library does not link it to the 1879 Remington. Single shift covers 461 machines, and where the shift is known its share rises from 32 of 78 in the 1890s to 154 of 157 in the 1930s.
- **Double shift.** Three rows of keys, three types per bar, and separate shifts for capitals and figures. Brooks's [US463724](../patents/US463724.md) (1891) stops the platen at three positions. There are 128 double-shift machines, nearly all from 1890 to 1929, including the Adler 7, the Olivers and the early portables. The [Adler 15](../machines/adler-15.md) (1909), Adler's first single-shift machine, was built "for typists who would not adapt to double shift".

Shifting is the commonest subject of the read typebar patents. [Shift by moving the platen](../features/shift-platen.md) has 506 patents and [Shift lock](../features/shift-lock.md) has 146. They range from a knee lever to hold the shift down ([US284231](../patents/US284231.md), 1883) to the [shift lock](../views/typewriter-features.md#shift-lock) (1891). Later machines moved the light type segment instead of the heavy carriage. The Lexikon notes this segment shift on the [Titania 2](../machines/titania-2.md) (1911), the [Torpedo 6](../machines/torpedo-6.md) (1927), the [Torpedo 15](../machines/torpedo-15-15-a-16.md) (1931, the first German portable with it) and Rheinmetall's [GS](../machines/gs.md) (1947).

**QWERTY against Ideal.** 573 typebar machines have a "QWERTY-type keyboard", a class that probably includes national variants. [QWERTY](../views/typewriter-features.md#qwerty-keyboard) became standard in the 1900s. Its main rival, the "Ideal" layout with DHIATENSOR on one row, belongs mostly to the Hammond (a type-element machine). Only 27 typebar machines have it, such as the [Crown](../machines/crown-rund.md) and the British [English](../machines/english.md) (1890). The library also tags several pre-1870 prototypes as "Ideal", which probably means only "not QWERTY", so treat that tag with care. After the war the [Princess 200](../machines/princess-200.md) (1954) adopted the German DIN standard keyboard.

## Inside the mechanism: basket, segment, bearings and guides

Understrike machines hung their bars in a ring, the basket. Front-strike machines set them in a slotted segment, one slot and pivot per bar. German makers made the set exchangeable for other alphabets: the [Adler 8](../machines/adler-8.md) (1902, for example Latin and Cyrillic), the [Mercedes 2](../machines/mercedes-2.md) (1908, Russian, Greek or Hebrew) and the [Mentor 2](../machines/mentor-2.md) (1912). Most machines put an intermediate lever between key and bar, as Wagner did. The [Glashütte](../machines/glashutte-modell-1922.md) (1922) advertised doing without one.

Dozens of pivots have to stay free but without play. John Edlund's [US883393](../patents/US883393.md) (1908) folds a typebar from sheet metal and mounts it on an adjustable ball-bearing hub in a slotted segment. The Lexikon calls the [Titania 2](../machines/titania-2.md) (1911) the first German machine with ball-bearing type levers. L. C. Smith is known in general history for ball-bearing typebars. The library links Carl Gabrielson's type-lever patent [US750274](../patents/US750274.md) (1904) to it with high confidence, but that patent has not been read.

The last millimetres before the platen decide alignment. Franz Strauss's [US981895](../patents/US981895.md) (1911) guides each bar only in the second half of its stroke. German patents add a roller running onto a guide plate ([DE349015](../patents/DE349015.md), 1922), a spur on the bar head ([DE375720](../patents/DE375720.md), 1923) and polished guide rails reaching about 40 mm from the printing point ([DE636150](../patents/DE636150.md), 1936). [DE518524](../patents/DE518524.md) (1931) runs two rows of typebars through the same guides. Postwar machines such as the [GSE](../machines/gse.md) (1952) had a "typebar untangler" for jammed bars.

## Ribbon, escapement and office controls

These controls are common to all keyboard typewriters, and the [features view](../views/typewriter-features.md) gives the first patents. They include the [ink ribbon](../views/typewriter-features.md#ink-ribbon) ([US65807](../patents/US65807.md), 1867), [margin stops](../views/typewriter-features.md#margin-stops-and-release) ([US349349](../patents/US349349.md), 1886), the [tabulator](../views/typewriter-features.md#tabulator) ([US439544](../patents/US439544.md), 1890), the [backspace](../views/typewriter-features.md#backspace) ([US589387](../patents/US589387.md), 1897), the [two-colour ribbon](../views/typewriter-features.md#two-colour-ribbon) ([US787566](../patents/US787566.md), 1905) and Gabrielson's [decimal tabulator](../views/typewriter-features.md#decimal-tabulator) ([US858713](../patents/US858713.md), 1907, linked to the L. C. Smith only with low confidence). The machine notes show them reaching real machines. The [Regina 1](../machines/regina-2.md) (1904) lacked a back-space key, and the [Regina 2](../machines/regina-2.md) added one in 1905. The 1909 advertisement for the [Mercedes 2](../machines/mercedes-2.md) lists two-colour ribbon, ribbon reverse, a decimal tabulator and back space. Most escapements use a rack and pawls ([Pawl-and-rack escapement](../features/esc-pawl-rack.md)). Glenn Barrett's two-pawl escapement [US697430](../patents/US697430.md) (1902) is linked with high confidence to the Fox machines.

## Portables

The [Corona](../machines/corona-3.md) of 1912 (Otto Petermann, $50, folding, three rows, double shift) is the library's best-documented portable. It is the top machine under [Portable or compact](../features/frm-compact-portable.md) and has 22 linked patents. German portables came at the same time: the folding [Albus](../machines/albus-1.md) (1909, about 2.5 kg), the [Erika 1](../machines/erika-1-2.md) (1910), the [Perkeo](../machines/perkeo-1.md) (1912, 2.6 kg) and the [Stoewer Elite](../machines/stoewer-elite.md) (1912, about 20,000 made). In the 1920s portables gained the four-row keyboard with single shift: the [Remington of 1920](../machines/monarch-pioneer.md), the [Klein-Stoewer](../machines/klein-stoewer.md) (1926), the [Erika 5](../machines/erika-5.md) (1927) and the [Klein-Triumph](../machines/modell-1-klein-triumph.md) (1928). Patents on [portable machines](../views/typewriter-features.md#portable-machines) peak in that decade. Later makers competed on height: the [Continental 200](../machines/continental-200.md) (1939, 9.5 cm), the [Princess](../machines/princess-model-1.md) (1949, about 6.5 cm), the [Gossen Tippa](../machines/gossen-tippa.md) (1949, 5.7 cm) and Olympia's [SF](../machines/sf.md) (1956, 7.5 cm).

## Electrics

Electric typebar machines came early and succeeded late. The Lexikon records an [electric Germania](../machines/electric-germania.md) shown in Paris in 1900, powered by an accumulator inside the type basket. It also records six [Hefner-Alteneck electric typebar machines](../machines/hefner-alteneck-electric-typebar.md) built around 1902 that never went into production. Joseph Ronchetti's [US888934](../patents/US888934.md) (1908) drives each bar with a solenoid. The [Mercedes Elektra](../machines/elektra-elektra-2-3.md) (1921) let a light touch release the bar while a motor made the stroke. The design that lasted was the [Electromatic](../machines/electromatic.md) (from 1930, IBM's from 1933), which the Lexikon calls the first machine with every function electrically triggered. The [IBM Executive](../machines/ibm-executive.md) (from 1952) added proportional spacing. Its 1954 German price of 2,670 DM was about that of a small car. German electrics followed: the [GSE](../machines/gse.md) (1952, nearly 120,000 built), the [Torpedo electric](../machines/torpedo-electric.md) (1957) and Olympia's [SGE](../machines/sge-10-20-30-monika.md) (1959). The [Electric drive](../features/imp-electric.md) feature has only 40 patents, mostly for type-wheel machines, so the machines tell this story better than the patents here.

## Makers and countries

The library has 405 typebar machines from Germany, 288 from the USA, 34 from England, 18 each from Italy and France, 10 from Switzerland and 7 from Sweden. America leads in the 1890s (64 of 87) and narrowly in the 1900s (66 American, 64 German). After that Germany leads: 103 to 47 in the 1920s and 111 to 35 in the 1930s. The Lexikon's German focus accounts for part of this. The counts below are per company page. Firm names in the source are often OCR-damaged, so one firm can be split over two pages, and the figures are approximate.

## Notes on the data

- Kind, strike, shift and keyboard come from the Lexikon via the machine pages, and a few classifications are debatable. The Oliver (U-shaped type bows) and the Malling-Hansen writing ball (a rod per character) are counted here as typebar machines, the Hammond as a type-element machine, the Noiseless as "unknown", and a few typebar pages are special machines. The counts may therefore be off by a few.
- The US patent set thins out after about 1915. Most of the patent evidence for later years is German.
